# Literature Review

This chapter reviews the five bodies of work the dissertation draws on: reinforcement learning for trading; market regimes and supervised regime classification; the evaluation of trading strategies; reward design and abstention; and the measurement of exposure in fund evaluation. It ends by stating the gap this dissertation addresses. Chapter 5 later compares the results with the closest studies in detail; this chapter sets out the background those comparisons rely on.

## Reinforcement Learning for Trading

Reinforcement learning (RL) studies an agent that learns by trial and error. At each step the agent observes a state, chooses an action and receives a reward. Its goal is to learn a policy, a rule for choosing actions, that maximises the expected sum of future rewards (Sutton and Barto, 2018). Trading fits this framework naturally. The state can be recent prices and indicators, the action a buy, sell or hold decision, and the reward a profit measure. The appeal is that the agent can learn a trading rule directly, without the designer first predicting prices.

Early work showed the idea could work. Moody and Saffell (2001) trained agents by "direct reinforcement", optimising a risk-adjusted performance measure (the differential Sharpe ratio) rather than predicting prices, and included transaction costs in the objective. Deep learning then made it possible to learn from richer inputs. Mnih et al. (2015) showed that deep Q-networks could reach human-level play in Atari games from raw pixels, and that result drove a wave of deep RL applications. In finance, Deng et al. (2017) combined deep feature learning with direct reinforcement for trading, and Jiang, Xu and Liang (2017) applied deep RL to cryptocurrency portfolio management. Open-source libraries such as FinRL (Liu et al., 2020) later made such agents easy to build and compare.

Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) is now one of the most widely used algorithms in this work (Schulman et al., 2017). PPO limits how far the policy can change in each update, which makes training more stable than earlier policy-gradient methods. It handles discrete action sets well, which suits the routing problem in this dissertation, where the agent chooses among a small set of trading engines and sizes.

Reported results are mixed. Several studies report agents that beat buy-and-hold. Felizardo et al. (2022) report gains on cryptocurrency assets; Amirzadeh et al. (2023) report a causal-RL PPO agent with a positive return on ETH; Gort et al. (2022) report that PPO trained with combinatorial purged cross-validation outperforms agents trained with simpler schemes; and Xue and Ye (2025) report PPO beating buy-and-hold on the S&P 500. Other studies find little or no edge. Kochliaridis et al. (2023) report PPO variants that did not beat buy-and-hold on cryptocurrency, and Ma (2025) reports that simple myopic optimisation outperformed RL in portfolio management. Théate and Ernst (2021) found that a deep Q-learning trader performed well on some stocks and poorly on others, and argued for more rigorous and standardised testing. The survey by Hambly, Xu and Yang (2023) covers RL across optimal execution, portfolio optimisation, market making and other financial problems, and shows how widely the settings, rewards and evaluation methods vary between studies. That variety makes results hard to compare.

That dependence is a known problem in RL generally. Henderson et al. (2018) showed that deep RL results can change substantially with the random seed, the implementation and the hyper-parameters, so that small differences between methods are often not reliable. Agarwal et al. (2021) showed that the common practice of reporting a few runs with point estimates can mislead, and recommended interval estimates and robust aggregate measures. These warnings matter for trading, where data are limited and noisy. They also shape the method of this dissertation, which reports results across seeds and states the statistical power of every comparison.

## Market Regimes and Supervised Regime Classification

Financial markets do not behave the same way all the time. Periods of calm, steady trends and sharp falls follow one another, and the best strategy differs between them. Hamilton (1989) formalised this idea with a Markov-switching model, in which the economy moves between hidden states with different statistical behaviour. Ang and Timmermann (2012) review the evidence that returns, volatility and correlations differ markedly across such regimes in many asset classes, and that investors who account for regimes can make better decisions.

This is the logic behind regime-switched trading. A grid strategy, which buys below and sells above a reference price, tends to profit in ranging markets but loses when prices trend strongly in one direction. A trend-following strategy does the opposite: it gains in sustained moves and loses through repeated false signals when markets range. Time-series momentum, the tendency of an asset's recent return to continue, is well documented across asset classes (Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012) and underpins trend following. Choosing the right engine for the current regime is therefore a natural problem for both supervised learning and RL.

Machine-learning classifiers are commonly used to label regimes from technical features. Random forests (Breiman, 2001) are a strong choice for noisy tabular data. They average many decision trees, each trained on a random sample of rows and features, which reduces overfitting and requires little tuning. Their raw outputs, however, are often poor probability estimates. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) showed that random forests and boosted trees produce distorted probabilities, and that calibration methods such as isotonic regression correct this. Zadrozny and Elkan (2002) extended calibration to multi-class problems, and Guo et al. (2017) showed that modern neural networks are also badly calibrated. Calibration matters whenever a predicted probability is used to make a decision, as it is in the supervised baseline of this dissertation, whose calibrated regime predictions decide which trading engine may run.

Cryptocurrency markets add their own features. Liu and Tsyvinski (2021) found that cryptocurrency returns are driven mainly by crypto-specific factors, such as momentum and investor attention, rather than by the factors that explain stock returns. Makarov and Schoar (2020) documented large and recurring price differences between exchanges, which shows that these markets can be segmented and less efficient than mature markets. Both findings support studying crypto assets as a distinct setting rather than assuming results from equities carry over.

## Evaluating Trading Strategies

Testing a trading strategy on past data, known as backtesting, is easy to do badly. The most serious risk is overfitting: if many strategies or settings are tried on the same data, some will look good by chance. Bailey et al. (2017) proposed a way to estimate the probability that a backtest is overfitted. Bailey and López de Prado (2014) introduced the deflated Sharpe ratio, which corrects a strategy's Sharpe ratio for the number of trials and for non-normal returns. Harvey and Liu (2015) argued that reported Sharpe ratios should be "haircut" in a similar way, and White (2000) proposed a "reality check" test for data snooping across many models.

A second risk is leakage, when information from the test period reaches the model during training. Financial data are ordered in time and strongly dependent, so the usual random train-test split is unsafe. López de Prado (2018) describes walk-forward testing, in which a model is trained on the past and tested on the period that follows, and the use of purging and an embargo, a gap between training and test data, to stop leakage through overlapping features. He also proposes combinatorial purged cross-validation, which produces more test paths from the same data. This dissertation uses walk-forward testing with an embargo, and Section 3.4 explains why it was preferred to the combinatorial scheme.

A third issue is statistical testing. Comparing two strategies on the same data requires tests that allow for dependence over time. Diebold and Mariano (1995) proposed a test for comparing forecast accuracy, and Newey and West (1987) gave standard errors that remain valid when observations are autocorrelated. Bootstrap methods for dependent data resample blocks of observations rather than single points (Politis and Romano, 1994), with the block length chosen from the data's dependence (Politis and White, 2004). When several comparisons are made, a correction such as Holm's (1979) controls the chance of a false positive.

Statistical power, the chance that a test detects an effect of a given size, is often ignored. Hoenig and Heisey (2001) showed that "observed power", computed after the fact from the observed effect, adds nothing beyond the p-value and should not be used to interpret a null result. Gelman and Carlin (2014) proposed design analysis instead: for a plausible true effect, calculate how likely a study is to get the sign wrong (a Type S error) or to exaggerate the size (a Type M error). These ideas are central to this dissertation, which finds that its comparison cannot detect effects smaller than tens of percentage points (Section 4.3).

## Reward Design, Reward Misspecification and Abstention

An RL agent optimises the reward it is given, not the goal its designer had in mind. The choice of reward is therefore one of the most important decisions in any RL system. Ng, Harada and Russell (1999) showed that only a specific form of reward shaping, based on a potential function, is guaranteed to leave the best policy unchanged. Other changes to the reward can change what the agent learns, sometimes in ways the designer does not expect.

When the reward differs from the true goal, agents often find unintended ways to score well. Amodei et al. (2016) listed "reward hacking" among the main practical problems of AI safety. Krakovna et al. (2020) collected many examples of this "specification gaming", in which agents satisfy the letter of the objective while defeating its purpose. Skalse et al. (2022) gave a formal definition of reward hacking and showed that a proxy reward which can never be "hacked" is very hard to construct. Karwowski et al. (2024) studied the same issue as Goodhart's law: optimising an imperfect proxy beyond a certain point lowers performance on the true goal. Trading rewards are proxies of this kind. They combine profit with penalties for costs and risk, and the balance between these terms decides what behaviour is optimal.

One way to see what drives an agent's behaviour is to split the reward into its parts. Lin et al. (2019; 2020) developed reward decomposition, which learns or reports the contribution of each reward component separately. This makes it visible when one term, such as a penalty, dominates the others.

Doing nothing is a special case of this problem. In some settings, not acting is the best choice and is designed as such. Classification with a reject option lets a model abstain when it is unsure (Chow, 1970), and selective prediction extends this to deep networks while reporting coverage, the share of cases on which the model does act (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017; Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2019). In market making, Wang, Ventre and Polukarov (2025) showed that occasionally refusing to quote can improve returns and Sharpe ratios. In these cases abstention is designed, measured and limited.

Abstention can also appear without being designed. Mohl et al. (2025) observed that market-making agents in a high-frequency setting learned to trade very rarely, and noted that under their reward an optimal policy seemed to be never to trade. Liu et al. (2023) described "lazy agents" in multi-agent RL that do nothing while teammates earn the reward. Offline RL methods such as conservative Q-learning (Kumar et al., 2020) deliberately make agents cautious, but this is a property of the learning algorithm, not of the reward. Several fields also use a "do-nothing" policy as a baseline. In power-grid control, every agent's score is measured relative to a do-nothing agent (Marot et al., 2021). In market making, a policy that never quotes is "a relatively strong benchmark" (Gašperov and Kostanjčar, 2021). In optimal execution, a passive policy is a standard comparison (Hafsi and Vittori, 2024), and Zhang (2025) uses an identically-zero hedge as a baseline. In the trading studies found, however, the do-nothing policy is compared on performance measures such as profit or Sharpe ratio. It is not scored under the agent's own training reward as a test of whether that reward is misspecified.

## Measuring Exposure: Lessons from Fund Evaluation

Fund managers have long been judged on how much risk they actually take, not only on the returns they report. Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986) separated a portfolio's return into the part due to asset allocation and the part due to security selection, so that managers are credited only for real decisions. Cremers and Petajisto (2009) introduced Active Share, which measures how far a fund's holdings differ from its benchmark; funds that hold close to the benchmark cannot claim much skill, whatever their returns. Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) showed that low-risk assets can earn high risk-adjusted returns, which means a portfolio can look skilful on a risk-adjusted measure simply by holding less risk.

The shared lesson is that two strategies should not be compared on risk or return without first knowing how much capital each one put to work. A strategy that is mostly in cash will show a small drawdown, but that says nothing about its skill. In trading-system evaluation this is easy to miss, because the usual activity measure, time in market, counts any bar on which a position or engine is active, however small. Capital-weighted exposure, the average share of equity actually deployed, is the measure that corresponds to the fund-evaluation practice above.

## Summary and Research Gap

Table 2.1 summarises the strands reviewed and what each contributes to this dissertation.

Table 2.1: Strands of literature and their role in this dissertation

| Strand | Key works | What this dissertation takes from it |
|---|---|---|
| RL for trading | Moody and Saffell (2001); Schulman et al. (2017); Théate and Ernst (2021); Hambly, Xu and Yang (2023) | The PPO routing agent, and the expectation that results depend on reward and evaluation design |
| Reproducibility in RL | Henderson et al. (2018); Agarwal et al. (2021) | Reporting across seeds; interval estimates |
| Regimes and calibration | Hamilton (1989); Ang and Timmermann (2012); Breiman (2001); Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) | The calibrated Random-Forest regime baseline |
| Backtest evaluation | López de Prado (2018); Bailey et al. (2017); Harvey and Liu (2015) | Walk-forward testing with an embargo; caution about overfitting |
| Statistical testing and power | Newey and West (1987); Politis and Romano (1994); Hoenig and Heisey (2001); Gelman and Carlin (2014) | Dependence-robust tests; prospective power and design analysis |
| Reward misspecification | Ng, Harada and Russell (1999); Amodei et al. (2016); Skalse et al. (2022); Karwowski et al. (2024) | The explanation of why an agent can learn to do nothing |
| Abstention and do-nothing baselines | Chow (1970); Geifman and El-Yaniv (2017); Wang, Ventre and Polukarov (2025); Mohl et al. (2025); Marot et al. (2021) | The do-nothing comparison; reporting how often the agent acts |
| Exposure in fund evaluation | Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986); Cremers and Petajisto (2009); Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) | Capital-weighted exposure as the correct activity measure |

Three gaps emerge from this review. First, RL trading studies report outcome measures such as return, drawdown and Sharpe ratio, and sometimes time in market, but capital-weighted exposure is not a standard reported quantity. Selective prediction solved the equivalent problem by making coverage a routine part of reporting; RL trading evaluation has no such convention. Second, do-nothing baselines exist in several fields, but in the trading work found they are compared on performance measures, not on the agent's own training reward. Scoring the do-nothing policy under the training reward is a direct test of whether the reward rewards inactivity. Third, the no-trade behaviour that some studies observe is treated as a reward-design problem to be fixed. It is not treated as an evaluation problem: a case in which the standard measures make an agent that does almost nothing look like a careful risk manager.

This dissertation addresses these gaps with a single, fully documented case. It does not claim that reinforcement learning generally fails at trading. It shows how, in one deployed system, reward-induced abstention produced better-looking risk figures, why standard evaluation did not reveal it, and which simple checks, drawn from the fields reviewed above, would have caught it.
